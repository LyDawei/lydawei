# What I did before, why I left, and what I'm doing now

I've been a professional software developer for 11.5 years now. In school, I really thought memorizing algorithms and being able to rewrite sorting algorithms were a testament to my capabilities as a dev — something that would tell companies, "I'm a good dev." It's funny how companies can steer you in the wrong direction. What I've found more valuable as a developer is curiosity, a drive to learn, a willingness to work with others, and pulling your weight.

When I first started my career, I was a consultant at a company called Avanade. I worked with various clients ranging from medical and oil & gas to social media companies. Afterwards, I left and worked in biotech, fintech, and finally at a software company that built software for oil & gas companies. Yeah, I jumped around a bit early on — mostly out of unhappiness, though the pay upgrades were a nice byproduct of it.

## Finding my footing as a developer

As a developer who worked in the pre-AI space, what did I enjoy? I enjoyed actually writing code. I went from backend development to embracing the chaos of JavaScript in its ReactJS form back around 2016. It was chaotic to be in that language space, but it allowed me to grow and embrace front-end development. Still, I didn't believe I was a good developer. I had C#, T-SQL, and .NET under my belt, but despite my manager telling me to move to New York and work at a startup because people would pay me $$$ to write ReactJS code, I just never felt good enough.

When I worked as a consultant, I had a manager named Federico. Federico is now a great friend of mine, and back when I was a junior, he gave me a project review that stuck with me: "David knows how to boost morale even when we're under pressure." I didn't get it at the time. Despite Federico always telling me, "It's easy to teach people technical skills; it's difficult to teach soft skills," I just couldn't connect the dots. I was constantly thinking, "This has nothing to do with software development." So while I was great socially, I constantly pushed myself to code more.

That was my first mistake — I never built anything useful for myself or my peers. I coded for the sake of coding. It was nonsense. Nothing was ever loved. I wrote things just to throw them away and prove that I could write them. APIs? Meh. Data structures? Anything beyond a linked list bored me. I wasn't coding intentionally; I was just coding reflexively.

## Lessons from toxic environments

When I left consulting, I landed a job in biotech because I wanted exposure to database design and development. It was purely for the sake of learning. I'd already done plenty of backend work, and frontend was easy at that point because I had ReactJS under my belt (even though the company was still using AngularJS at the time). The lead even told me, "You are clearly not a junior developer." That validation felt good — but the environment didn't. I socially degraded, got caught with my guard down, and was unprofessional in ways I regret — all due to the toxic nature of the workplace. I was reactive to it, and the only fix was to leave. So, with my new skills under my belt, I moved on.

Next came fintech — a company that handled traffic toll systems. The debugging and database design skills I'd built helped me succeed, but the honeymoon phase ended quickly. The technical side felt "complete" from A to B, and everything else was just rewriting the wheel or chasing new tech around the stack. It seemed like I was finally becoming a "senior" developer — but I still didn't feel it. What I did learn was what a bad leader looked like.

The director I worked under lied to me from the beginning. He promised a bonus that didn't exist and only later corrected my salary. The "bonus" system was arbitrary, given to whoever he wanted, and the culture felt like indentured servitude disguised as "salaried professionalism." He was also a yes-man — which, in development, is disastrous. A yes-man isn't inherently bad, but one who doesn't communicate changes in deadlines or expectations is absolutely toxic. He'd agree to new demands last minute, claim "We're agile," but still expect the same deadline. Excuse me, sir — do you order a cheeseburger, tell them to take off the cheese after it's cooked, and still expect it on time?

It all came to a breaking point in late 2019. My grandmother was hospitalized, and I took an emergency trip home to see her before she passed. I tried working remotely on my personal laptop, but the setup didn't work. When I requested bereavement leave, they gave me less time than I needed, so I used PTO instead. When I returned, I could tell my director didn't care — in fact, he looked at me like I was lying. That was the final straw. I told him he agrees to things too much and pivots without warning. His response? "Well, you know what they say: the customer is always right."

So I left.

## Rediscovering meaning

The next gig — at a software company — was the best I've had so far. I learned more there than anywhere else. The people were amazing: talented, wise, and grounded. They didn't just share textbook knowledge — they shared hard-earned wisdom from real experience. There was still new tech to learn (Salesforce), but everything else was familiar. The director? Brilliant. He corrected my salary to what I deserved, trusted my process, and even embraced my chaos.

The people there helped me grow technically without me having to learn new tools. They helped me refine my thinking. I finally understood that being a developer isn't about how much code you write — it's about how you think, communicate, and collaborate. But even then, I faced the lingering question: what have I built to prove I'm a senior dev? As a hobbyist, I still had nothing to show.

## Enter AI

Then AI came along — a force multiplier for insanity. The old adage "Garbage in, garbage out" still applies. But I've been embracing AI as a dev tool. It works until it doesn't, but it works enough. Now, I can finally use the limited time I have each day to build things that prove I'm a senior dev.

"But you didn't write it," some might say. You're right — I didn't. But I also didn't write the entire websites for Match.com or Frontier Communications. I didn't reimplement the fintech behind TransCore. I kind of reimplemented the field service application for PTC, but at the end of the day, it's not about doing it all by yourself. It's about everything you've seen: the infrastructure, the tools, knowing when to buy versus build, watching trends rise and fall, and seeing who paid dearly for chasing them. I've built lasting relationships with people far smarter than me — and they've all taught me how to be a better developer.

## Where I am now

I left PTC and joined a company powered by AI tools. I'm learning a lot — mostly through mistakes. Sometimes I lean too heavily on AI without proper thought, or I get lazy and overlook implementation details that matter. The problem? When those mistakes hit, they hit hard. You look like a buffoon who can't figure out the code you wrote — and `git blame` has your name all over it.

## TL;DR — Be intentional

If there's one takeaway from this entire story, it's this: **be intentional with your coding**. For things like UI/UX, sure, be flexible — but business logic must be deliberate. You need to pay attention to what's happening under the hood.

AI frees you to see the bigger picture — to focus on design, structure, and systems instead of syntax. But that freedom comes with a new burden: **attention**. You have to pay attention to what you're working on and to the weight of each decision.

That's what separates "just coding" from building something that lasts.
